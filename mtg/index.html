Atlas legend & card terms

W = White · U = Blue · B = Black · R = Red · G = Green. A diagonal entry is one color’s toolkit, not a second copy of that color.

Aggro

Spend early turns adding pressure. Cheap creatures and ways through blockers matter more than a huge late-game card.

Tempo

An attacker keeps dealing damage while bounce, tap effects, or counterspells delay the opponent. Those spells need an attacker to turn the extra time into damage.

Midrange

Efficient creatures and extra value help you win after early trades. Defend against aggro and attack against control.

Control

Early defense and resource trades keep you alive while card draw puts you ahead. You also need a reliable finisher.

Ramp

Invest in extra mana to play larger threats sooner. Draft both acceleration and good cards to spend that mana on.

Synergy / combo

Cards with synergy work better together. Most draft engines generate repeated value; an infinite combo needs specific cards that may not be in your pool.

Go wide / tokens

Make several creatures rather than one large threat. Creature tokens are made by other cards.

Blink / ETB

Exile a creature and return it to reuse its enters-the-battlefield (ETB) ability.

Sacrifice / fodder / outlet

Fodder is a creature you can afford to lose. An outlet lets you sacrifice it; a payoff rewards doing so.

Attrition / recursion

Trade resources over time. Recursion brings cards back from the graveyard for another use.

Burn / reach

Burn deals direct damage. Reach here means damage that can finish an opponent without a successful attack; it is not the creature keyword reach.

Stompy / counters

Stompy uses efficient, aggressive creatures. Counter plans grow creatures with +1/+1 counters; counterspells instead stop spells.

Fixing / splash

Fixing helps produce the colors you need. A splash adds a few cards of a third color.

Payoff / enabler / curve

An enabler makes a strategy work; a payoff rewards it. Your curve is the spread of your cards’ mana costs.

Strategy definitions: aggro, tempo, midrange, and control. The pair plans use common color tools as examples. Your cube or set may support different themes.

Adding a third color

A third color should improve your existing plan enough to justify harder mana. Start with two main colors; splash a small number of powerful, later-game cards when the fixing supports it.

Choose one of the ten combinations to see possible overlaps and base pairs.

Does it solve a problem?

A strong removal spell or finisher may earn a splash. A medium creature usually won’t. Don’t splash an early two-drop that must be cast on time.

Can you cast it?

Prefer one splash-colored symbol. Double symbols demand much more support. A green fixer needs green mana to cast it. Card draw alone does not fix your colors.

What does it cost?

Every off-color basic can weaken your main colors. Tapped lands can slow aggression. You also need to spend draft picks on fixing.

A practical mana check

For a small splash with one colored symbol that you only need late, start with roughly 3 to 4 reliable sources. That does not guarantee you can cast it; you need more sources if you need the color early or repeatedly. Count usable dual lands and accessible fixing, and check the main colors’ early and double-symbol costs too.

True three-color decks are possible when a pool is built for them. Without that support, two colors are a safer baseline. See Building a Mana Base.

Choosing cards during the draft

Ask what a card does in your deck, how reliably you can cast it, and whether the cards needed around it are reaching you.

Early picks

Take strong, flexible cards. Efficient creatures, good removal, and broadly useful value cards leave room to find a lane. You can change colors after an early pick.

Middle picks

Repeated good cards in a color suggest room to draft it. Compare the strength of what’s arriving with your current pool. One late gold card may mean only that nobody has that exact pair.

Late picks

Prioritize missing roles: two-drops, interaction, engine support, fixing, or a finisher. A useful on-plan common can beat a stronger card you won’t cast or won’t play.

Your six-person table

Trust repeated useful cards more than weak leftovers. A card wheeling is evidence of demand at this table, not proof its color is universally open. Small pods make niche engines less predictable.

Before the next draft

Ask the host which pair themes the pool supports, how much fixing is included, and whether the list is available. Each theme needs several enablers as well as a payoff. In an all-common cube, you may not have the gold uncommon “signposts” found in retail sets.

Check the finished deck

Start around 17 lands; adjust for curve, ramp, card draw, and mana sinks. A simple two-color 9/8 basic split is only a baseline: early colored costs can demand a different split.

Checking a pick

A card should be strong in your deck, fill a role you need, and be castable on time.

Removal isn’t automatically better than a needed threat. Life gain needs a payoff or a reason to buy time. Bounce needs pressure or meaningful tempo. Synergy needs enough actual cards.

Scope, sources & what depends on your pool

“Pauper draft” is treated here as commons-focused Limited, often a cube. Official constructed Pauper uses 60-card decks; normal draft uses a 40-card minimum. This guide assumes normal Limited deckbuilding and passing. Your exact list and house rules are unknown. One-on-one is the initial match context, and six drafters is an adjustable illustration within your 4 to 8 range.

Color tools draw on Wizards’ color pie. The pair plans, support checks, three-color overlaps, practice picks, and multiplayer advice are examples. They may not match the archetypes or strongest decks in your pool. Some themes such as counters, auras, artifacts, lifegain, mill, and tribal decks only work when enough suitable cards are included.
